Cetacea

A proof checker for people who are learning to prove things.

Open the editor

What this is

Cetacea is a proof checker. You write a proof one step per line, and it reads along behind you and tells you, at every step, what you have, what you still owe, and whether the step you just took was legal. When it says accepted, the proof is right. Not "probably right," not "the grader didn't notice" — right.

It runs entirely in your browser. There is nothing to install and no account to make, and your proofs never leave your computer.

This page is a tour of the tool: where things are on the screen, how to write a proof, how to read the checker when it says no, and what all the menus are for. It is not a logic course. That's the job of the book, Proofs, Checked, which takes the slow, friendly path through the mathematics and uses this tool the whole way. Read this first, though, and the book will go more smoothly.

The whole page takes about twenty minutes, and it goes best with the editor open in another tab. Every complete example below has an Open in the editor link that does exactly that.

The screen

Here is the editor, drawn as a map:

You type on the left. Everything on the right is the checker talking back, and it talks back constantly: a moment after you stop typing, it has re-checked the whole file. (The Check button is there for when you want to be sure, but you will rarely need it.)

  • The status line at the top right is the verdict on the whole file: how many theorems are accepted, how many are incomplete, how many failed.
  • Goals shows where you are in the proof your cursor is in. This is the panel you will look at most.
  • Diagnostics is where errors appear. Click one and the cursor jumps to the line.
  • Suggested tactics, Proof explanation, Theorem library and Tactic reference are help of various kinds. We'll meet each one below.

Your first proof

Let's prove something small: if you know P and Q, then you know Q and P. Here is the whole thing. 

theorem and_swap (P Q : Prop) : P /\ Q -> Q /\ P := by
  intro h
  split
  exact h.right
  exact h.left

The first line is the claim, and it has four parts:

  • theorem and_swap gives the theorem a name. The name is yours to choose.
  • (P Q : Prop) says what the theorem is about: two propositions, P and Q. They could be anything — "it is raining," "the bus is late" — and the proof will work for all of them.
  • P /\ Q -> Q /\ P is the statement being proved.
  • := by says "and here is the proof."

Everything after that, indented, is the proof: a list of tactics, one per line. A tactic is a single move — "assume the premise," "prove the two halves separately" — and the checker carries out each one and keeps track of what's left.

The symbols are spelled so that you can type them:

You meanYou typeAlso accepted
P and QP /\ QP ∧ Q
P or QP \/ QP ∨ Q
not Pnot P¬P
if P then QP -> QP → Q
P if and only if QP <-> QP ↔ Q
for every xforall x : T, …
for some xexists x : T, …

Now watch the proof happen. At every moment the checker holds a goal: the things you have (your hypotheses, each with a name) above a dashed line, and the thing you still have to prove (the target) below it, after a ⊢. Each tactic changes the goal. Here is the goal after each line:

theorem and_swap …
⊢ P /\ Q -> Q /\ P
intro h
h : P /\ Q
⊢ Q /\ P
split
h : P /\ Q
[1] ⊢ Q
h : P /\ Q
[2] ⊢ P
exact h.right
h : P /\ Q
⊢ P
exact h.left
No goals remain.

In words:

  1. You start with nothing in hand, owing the whole statement.
  2. intro h handles the ->. To prove "if A then B," you assume A and prove B, so the premise moves above the line and gets the name you gave it, h.
  3. split handles the /\ in the target. To prove "Q and P" you prove each half, so one goal becomes two.
  4. exact h.right closes the first goal. h is a proof of P /\ Q, so h.right is a proof of Q, which is exactly what was asked for.
  5. exact h.left closes the second the same way, and there is nothing left to prove.

The status line now reads 1 accepted · logic fol, mode constructive. That's it; that's a checked proof.

Living in the Goals panel

You do not have to keep that table of goals in your head, because the Goals panel is that table. Put your cursor on any line of a proof, and the panel shows the goal after that line. Put it on the theorem line and you see where you started.

Try it now: open the proof above, click on the split line, and then move down one line at a time with the arrow keys. You'll see two goals become one and then none.

This is how proofs actually get written. Nobody types five tactics and then looks up. You type one, glance right, see what you owe now, and decide on the next. If you ever feel lost, the question to ask is always the same: what does the Goals panel say?

When there are several goals, tactics work on the first one. You finish it, and the next goal moves up to take its place.

When the checker says no

It will say no a lot. That's normal, and it's the point: a wrong step is caught the moment you make it, on the line where you made it. Here is the same proof with the last two lines swapped. 

theorem and_swap (P Q : Prop) : P /\ Q -> Q /\ P := by
  intro h
  split
  exact h.left
  exact h.right

Line 4 turns red, and the Diagnostics panel says:

error · line 4
theorem `and_swap` failed: proof has type `P`, but expected `Q`
  • h : P /\ Q
  • target: Q

Read it slowly, because every error has this shape. The first line is what went wrong: you handed over a proof of P (that's what h.left is), and what was wanted was a proof of Q. Underneath is the goal at the moment it went wrong: what you had, and what the target was. Nearly always, the fix is sitting in those lines.

The proof that stops early

If you delete the last line instead, every step you took is fine. There just aren't enough of them:

theorem and_swap (P Q : Prop) : P /\ Q -> Q /\ P := by
  intro h
  split
  exact h.right
error · line 4
theorem `and_swap` failed: the proof is incomplete: 1 goal remains
  • h : P /\ Q
  • target: P

The checker tells you which goal is still open, which is to say, what to do next.

The goal that can't be proved

Sometimes the trouble isn't the line with the error. It's an earlier choice that painted you into a corner. Here someone has "or" in hand and tries to commit to one side too soon: 

theorem or_swap (P Q : Prop) : P \/ Q -> Q \/ P := by
  intro h
  left
  exact h
error · line 4
theorem `or_swap` failed: proof has type `P \/ Q`, but expected `Q`
  • h : P \/ Q
  • target: Q
  • the open goal does not follow from the current hypotheses: it is false when P = true, Q = false. Reconsider the earlier proof steps.

That last note is worth knowing about. The checker has noticed that the goal you're stuck on can't be proved from what you have, and it gives you a concrete situation where it fails. No amount of cleverness on line 4 will help; the mistake was left on line 3. (The right proof looks at h first. It's in the table of moves below.)

Typos

And sometimes you just mistyped. Write splt and the checker says unknown tactic `splt`; did you mean `split`? It does the same for misspelled theorem names. 

theorem and_swap (P Q : Prop) : P /\ Q -> Q /\ P := by
  intro h
  splt
  exact h.right
  exact h.left

Unfinished business: sorry

There is one tactic that closes any goal at all: sorry. It means "I'll come back to this." 

theorem and_swap (P Q : Prop) : P /\ Q -> Q /\ P := by
  intro h
  split
  sorry
  exact h.left

The checker goes along with it, but it doesn't forget. The theorem is not accepted; it is incomplete, and the status line says so: 1 incomplete · logic fol, mode constructive.

sorry is how you work on one part of a proof without the rest shouting at you. Sketch the outline, put sorry where the hard parts go, check that the outline holds together, and then come back and replace them one at a time. Exercise files arrive full of sorry for exactly this reason: the incomplete theorems are your to-do list.

Getting unstuck

Three of the panels on the right exist for the moment when you don't know what to type.

Suggest a tactic. Put the cursor on a line of a proof and press the button. The checker looks at the goal at that point and proposes moves that fit it. Each suggestion has actually been run before you see it, so nothing on the list is a guess that might not even apply. Click one to insert it. Use this the way you'd use a hint in the back of the book: after you've had a real try.

Proof explanation. With the cursor inside a theorem, this panel lists the proof step by step in plain words — "assume the premise," and so on — with the step you're on marked. It's handy for reading a proof somebody else wrote, including the book's.

Tactic reference. At the bottom of the right-hand side, every tactic is listed by what it's for. Click a row to open it.

The moves, on one page

Which tactic to use is almost always decided by the shape of what's in front of you. If the shape is in the target, you're trying to prove it; if it's in a hypothesis, you're trying to use it.

ShapeTo prove it (target)To use it (hypothesis h)
A -> Bintro happly h
A /\ Bsplith.left, h.right
A \/ Bleft or rightcases h with …
not Aintro hcontradiction
A <-> Bsplith.left, h.right
forall x, …intro xapply h
exists x, …exists tobtain x hx := h
a = breflrewrite -> h

And when the target is something you already have, exact h hands it over. Here is a proof that uses a few of these at once, including cases, which has a layout of its own: one indented arm for each way the "or" could be true. 

theorem or_swap (P Q : Prop) : P \/ Q -> Q \/ P := by
  intro h
  cases h with
  | left hp =>
      right
      exact hp
  | right hq =>
      left
      exact hq

One more tool deserves a mention, because longer proofs lean on it: have lets you prove a stepping stone, give it a name, and then use it. 

theorem chain (P Q R : Prop) : (P -> Q) -> (Q -> R) -> P -> R := by
  intro hpq
  intro hqr
  intro hp
  have hq : Q := by
    apply hpq
    exact hp
  apply hqr
  exact hq

Using what's already proved

You don't have to prove everything from nothing. Cetacea comes with a library of theorems about logic, numbers, sets, functions and lists, and one line at the top of your file brings it in: 

logic fol_nat

import std/prelude.ctea

theorem swap_sum (a b : Nat) : a + b = b + a := by
  exact add_comm

theorem swap_inside (a b c : Nat) : (a + b) + c = (b + a) + c := by
  rewrite -> add_comm {n := a; m := b}
  refl

A library theorem is used just like a hypothesis. In the first proof the target is precisely what add_comm says, so exact add_comm finishes it. In the second, add_comm is used to rewrite part of the target, and the braces say which n and m you mean, since there's more than one sum it could apply to.

How do you know add_comm exists? The Theorem library panel. Type comm or add into its search box and it lists every theorem whose name or statement matches, along with exactly what each one says. Searching there before proving a small fact yourself is a very good habit.

(The logic fol_nat line switches on the natural numbers. More on that below.)

Numbers, induction, and decide

Everything so far has been about Ps and Qs, but the same editor handles numbers, sets, functions, lists and data types you define yourself. Two things are worth seeing early.

Proofs about every natural number go by induction, which is laid out like cases: one arm for zero, one for the step from k to its successor, where you get an induction hypothesis to use. 

logic fol_nat

import std/prelude.ctea

theorem sub_self (n : Nat) : n - n = 0 := by
  induction n with
  | zero =>
      refl
  | succ k ih =>
      simp
      exact ih

theorem small_sum : 2 + 3 = 5 := by
  decide

And when a claim has no unknowns in it — it's about particular numbers, or particular values of a small data type — decide simply works it out. If the claim turns out to be false, it tells you which part.

The book spends whole chapters on each of these. This is just so you recognise them.

The menus

Logic

Cetacea can check three sizes of mathematics, and a file says which one it wants with a logic line at the top:

LineWhat you get
logic folPropositions and quantifiers. Enough for pure logic.
logic fol_natAll that, plus the natural numbers, arithmetic and induction.
logic holAll that, plus sets, functions, lists and your own data types.

The Logic menu is the setting for files that don't have such a line. If you write Nat in a file that hasn't asked for numbers, the checker tells you which line to add.

There is one more switch of this kind. Some ways of reasoning, like proof by contradiction, are classical, and files start out without them. Writing mode classical on a line of its own turns them on for the theorems after it. The book's Chapter 3 is about why anyone would care about the difference.

Section

If you're following the book or taking a course, the Section menu is the one that matters. Pick the chapter you're on, and the checker limits itself to the moves that chapter has taught. It sets the logic for you, too.

This is not there to be annoying. An exercise that's meant to make you practise case analysis teaches nothing if a bigger tactic from six chapters later does it in one line. So under Chapter 2's rules, this proof is turned away: 

theorem dne (P : Prop) : not not P -> P := by
  intro h
  by_contra hn
  contradiction
error · line 3
`by_contra` is not available in the section `Chapter 2: Natural deduction`
  • tactics available here: intro, exact, apply, split, trivial, assumption, show, left, right, cases, contradiction, exfalso, have

(Open it and you'll see a second error underneath, about classical mode. That's the other switch from a moment ago: by_contra is a classical move, and this file never asked for them.)

When a section is selected, a Section rules panel appears on the right listing exactly what's allowed, so you never have to guess. Choose "No section rules" to have everything available.

Open a book file

Every file that goes with the book is in this menu, chapter by chapter: the worked examples, the deliberately wrong proofs each chapter picks apart, the exercises, and the solutions. Opening one also sets the Section menu to its chapter.

Your work on each book file is remembered separately. You can leave the Chapter 4 exercises half done, go look at something in Chapter 2, and come back to find them as you left them. If you want a clean copy, the same menu grows a "Start … over" entry for the file you have open.

Save and Share

Save downloads what's in the editor as a file. Share copies a link to your clipboard that opens the editor with your file in it, section and all. That link is the easiest way to show somebody a proof, or to ask for help with one: paste it into an email or a discussion post and they see exactly what you see.

Your instructor may send you a link of the same kind for an assignment. Those open with an Assignment panel on the right, which lists the theorems you're asked to prove and keeps track of which ones are done.

Small things that trip people up

  • Your work is kept in this browser. Close the tab and come back, and the editor has what you left in it. But it lives in this browser on this computer, and clearing your browsing data clears it. Use Save or Share for anything you'd be sad to lose.
  • Opening a link replaces what's in the editor. That includes the "Open in the editor" links on this page. (Work on a book file is safe; it's stored with the file.)
  • Indentation matters. The lines of a proof are indented under the theorem, and the lines inside a cases or induction arm are indented further. Copy the layout of the examples and you'll be fine.
  • Comments start with two dashes. Anything after -- on a line is ignored by the checker. Write notes to yourself.
  • Hypothesis names are up to you. intro h and intro raining do the same thing. Pick names that help you read the Goals panel.
  • The very first check takes a moment. The library is loaded the first time a file asks for it. After that, checking is fast.
  • A red line isn't always the guilty line. The checker reports where it got stuck, which can be a step or two after the choice that caused it. Walk the cursor back up and watch the Goals panel.

Where to go next

  • Open the editor, load the first example, and finish the proof it leaves for you.
  • Read Proofs, Checked. Start at Chapter 1, pick "Chapter 1" in the Section menu, and open the chapter's files from the Book menu as you go.
  • If you'd rather work in a terminal, or you're an instructor with a stack of files to check, the same checker is a command-line program. It's in the repository, along with everything else.

One last thing. Rejection is the normal state of a proof in progress. Nobody's proofs go through the first time, so don't take a red line personally — just read what it says, and take the next step.
